1. THE MOM TEST (Rob Fitzpatrick)
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Principle: Focus on collecting concrete facts about customers' lives by discussing
specific past actions. Good interviewing relies on questions that even biased people
cannot lie about.

Core Rule: "Talk about their life instead of your idea"

Failure Modes:
• Inviting bad data through compliments, fluff (generics, hypotheticals, future), and ideas
• Heavy-handed questions that force people to say nice things
• Exposing ego or pitching solutions too early
• Accepting compliments (the "fool's gold" of customer learning)
• Asking about the future instead of the past
• Generic claims without specific examples

Detectable Textual Signals:
• ego-exposure/seeking-compliment: "do you think it's a good idea", "do you like it?",
  "what do you think of my solution?", "would this help you?"
• fluff-future-tense/fluff-hypothetical: "I would", "I will", "I might", "I could",
  "Would you ever buy...", "If we built this, would you..."
• fluff-generic-claim: "I usually", "I always", "I never", "people like", "everyone says"
• closed-question/request-for-opinion: Simple yes/no affirmation questions, price guesses
• pitching-solution: Describing your solution before understanding their problem
• leading-question: Questions that telegraph the "right" answer

Good Signals (praise these):
• past-behavior-good: "Talk me through the last time that happened", "When did you last...",
  "Can you walk me through your workflow for..."
• specific-example-request: "Can you give me a specific example?", "Tell me more about that time..."
• current-cost-inquiry: "How much does the problem cost you currently?", "What have you spent on..."

Bad → Good Examples:
✗ "Do you think it's a good idea?"
✓ "Talk me through the last time that happened"

✗ "How much would you pay for X?"
✓ "How much does the problem cost them currently? What budget do you have allocated for this?"

✗ "Would you buy a product which did X?"
✓ "How are you dealing with X now? What does that cost you in time/money?"

✗ "I've built a solution that does X. What do you think?"
✓ "What's the hardest part about [process]? Can you describe the last time you struggled with it?"

2. TALKING TO HUMANS (Giff Constable)
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Principle: Focus on meaningful dialogue where customer stories illuminate business
paths. Begin with open-ended questions to elicit authentic experiences and pain points.

Core Rule: "Let them tell stories, don't interrogate with yes/no questions"

Failure Modes:
• Jumping to pointed questions designed to get desired answers
• Relying on yes-or-no questions that limit dialogue depth
• Conducting text-based interviews that lose crucial non-verbal cues
• Talking to the wrong customer segment
• Not probing deeply enough on important signals

Detectable Textual Signals:
• closed-question: Interrogative sentences with yes/no answers ("Do you...", "Would you...", "Is it...")
• seeking-validation: Questions obviously steering toward confirming existing beliefs
• too-broad: Questions so general they don't yield actionable insights
• segment-mismatch: Talking to wrong persona/customer type
• no-evidence-ask: Accepting claims without asking for evidence
• missed-probe: Not following up on emotional signals or important statements

Good Signals (praise these):
• open-ended-elaboration: "Tell me about...", "How do you...", "What does... look like for you?"
• story-elicitation: "Can you walk me through...", "Describe a time when..."
• context-building: Questions that build understanding of their world

Bad → Good Examples:
✗ "Do you agree it's hard to research products?"
✓ "Can you tell me about a time you faced challenges researching similar products? What happened?"

✗ "Would you use this daily?"
✓ "What prevents you from completing that task efficiently today? Walk me through your current process."

✗ "Is email a good way to contact you?"
✓ "How do you prefer to receive updates about products you're evaluating? What works best in your workflow?"

✗ "You mentioned frustration - is that common?"
✓ "Tell me more about that frustration. What triggered it? How often does this happen?"

3. LEAN CUSTOMER DEVELOPMENT (Cindy Alvarez)
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Principle: Reduce business risks by challenging assumptions. Best predictor of future
behavior is current behavior. Listen for emotion as a sign of priority.

Core Rule: "What they do > what they say"

Failure Modes:
• Confirmatory bias - seeing only what confirms your assumptions
• Asking for feature lists (intellectual priority vs emotional priority mismatch)
• Accepting feature requests at face value without understanding underlying needs
• Missing emotional indicators that signal true pain points
• Not investigating current alternatives and workarounds
• Failing to understand constraints and buying process

Detectable Textual Signals:
• emotional-indicator-missing: Discussion of problems without any emotional language
• wishing-question: "If you could wave a magic wand...", "In an ideal world..."
• feature-request-superficial: Accepting "I'd like feature X" without asking why
• confirmatory-question: Questions designed to confirm what you already believe
• intellectual-vs-emotional: Rational feature requests without exploring emotional impact

Good Signals (praise these):
• seeking-current-behavior: "What tools do you use now?", "How often do you...",
  "Show me your current workflow..."
• cost-or-time-pain-check: "How much does this cost you?", "How much time do you spend..."
• alternatives-explored: "What else have you tried?", "What workarounds do you use?"
• constraint-investigation: "What would prevent you from...?", "Who else needs to approve?"
• emotion-acknowledged: Following up on frustration, excitement, anxiety signals
• priority-validation: "If you had to pick one thing to fix, what would it be?"

Bad → Good Examples:
✗ "You'd like [feature]?"
✓ "If we built this feature, what would you be able to do that you can't today? What does that unlock for you?"

✗ "What would you do with a magic wand?"
✓ "What's the one thing about [current process] that, if you could change it, would make the biggest difference? Why that specifically?"

✗ "Would you pay for this?"
✓ "What do you pay for similar solutions now? What's your budget for this category? Who controls that budget?"

✗ "What features do you want?"
✓ "What are you trying to accomplish? What's blocking you from doing that today?"

4. THE LEAN STARTUP (Eric Ries)
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Principle: Achieve validated learning through testable experiments. Test fundamental
leap-of-faith assumptions (Value Hypothesis: does it deliver value? Growth Hypothesis:
how will it grow?). Use actionable metrics over vanity metrics.

Core Rule: "Learn whether to pivot or persevere through validated learning"

Failure Modes:
• Building products customers refuse to use (no value hypothesis test)
• Achieving failure - successfully executing a flawed plan
• Relying on vanity metrics that give false impression of progress
• Optimization without learning - tweaking without testing hypotheses
• Not identifying or testing leap-of-faith assumptions

Detectable Textual Signals:
• vanity-metric-citation: "total registered users", "40,000 hits this month", "we have X downloads"
• optimization-without-learning: Technical improvements without hypothesis being tested
• achieving-failure: Celebrating technical execution while missing the customer problem
• no-hypothesis-stated: Changes made without clear prediction of outcome
• missing-experiment-design: Not discussing how to test assumptions

Good Signals (praise these):
• actionable-metric-focus: Conversion rates, retention cohorts, engagement metrics
• hypothesis-testing: "We believe that X will lead to Y", "Our assumption is..."
• mvp-learning-focus: "The minimum we need to test whether...", "This will tell us if..."
• pivot-or-persevere: Explicit discussions about what would trigger a change in strategy
• value-hypothesis-test: "Do customers actually want this?", "Will they pay for it?"
• growth-hypothesis-test: "How will we acquire more customers?", "What's our engine of growth?"

Bad → Good Examples:
✗ "We delivered the feature perfectly on schedule"
✓ "The MVP confirmed that users have a desire to [solve problem X], validating our value hypothesis"

✗ "We got record sign-ups this month!"
✓ "What percentage of last month's cohort is still actively engaged? What does that tell us about product-market fit?"

✗ "We're optimizing the load time"
✓ "We hypothesize that reducing load time will increase conversion by X%. How will we measure if we're right?"

✗ "Everyone loves the new design"
✓ "Has the redesign improved our activation rate? What does user behavior data show?"
